## 4. The Top 5 Golden Rules of Clean Hinglish

### Rule 1: Always Preserve the Aspirated 'h' (`jh`, `kh`, `chh`, `th`, `bh`)
In Hindustani phonology, aspirated consonants represent distinct letters (e.g., झ is not ज; छ is not च). Dropping the 'h' fundamentally changes the consonant quality:
- ❌ `muje` $\rightarrow$ ✅ **`mujhe`** (*"Mujhe thoda time dena"*)
- ❌ `tuje` $\rightarrow$ ✅ **`tujhe`** (*"Tujhe bhi aana hoga"*)
- ❌ `kuch` $\rightarrow$ ✅ **`kuchh`** (*"Kuchh nahi kar rahe"*)
- ❌ `samaj` / `samja` $\rightarrow$ ✅ **`samajh`** / **`samjha`** (*"Galat mat samajh"*)

### Rule 2: End First-Person Present Verbs with 'hoon' (`hu` → `hoon`)
The copula हूँ / ہوں is intrinsically long and nasalized. Writing `hu` feels abrupt and unrefined. Always use **`hoon`** (or `hun`):
- ❌ *"Me busy hu"* $\rightarrow$ ✅ *"Main busy **hoon**"*
- ❌ *"Me ghar par hu"* $\rightarrow$ ✅ *"Main ghar par **hoon**"*

### Rule 3: Use 'ai' for Pronouns & Past Actions (`mene` → `maine`)
The Hindi/Urdu diphthong ऐ / ئے (/ɛː/) is properly transliterated with `ai`. Using `e` makes Hindustani look like colloquial dialect reduction:
- ❌ `mene` $\rightarrow$ ✅ **`maine`** (*"Maine galti ki thi"*)
- ❌ `kese` $\rightarrow$ ✅ **`kaise`** (*"Kaise dekhe ek doosre ko?"*)
- ❌ `tune` $\rightarrow$ ✅ **`tu ne`** (*"Abhi to tu ne haan kaha"*)

### Rule 4: Retain the Plural & Interrogative Nasal 'n' (`baatein`, `kyun`, `kahan`)
Feminine plural nouns and question words end in an expressive nasal *noon ghunna* (ँ / ں). Always include the terminal 'n':
- ❌ `baate` $\rightarrow$ ✅ **`baatein`** (*"Hamari meethi baatein chalti rahegi"*)
- ❌ `kyu` $\rightarrow$ ✅ **`kyun`** (*"Kyun ignore kar rahi ho?"*)
- ❌ `kaha` $\rightarrow$ ✅ **`kahan`** (*"Aap kahan ho?"* Note: 'kaha' means 'said', 'kahan' means 'where!')

### Rule 5: Future First-Person Verbs Use 'oo' (`loonga`, `doonga`, `jaoonga`)
Verbal suffixes ending in -ूँगा / -وں گا carry a rich, full vowel. Using double 'o' conveys warmth, intent, and grammatical accuracy:
- ❌ `lunga` $\rightarrow$ ✅ **`loonga`** (*"Sab sambhal loonga"*)
- ❌ `dunga` $\rightarrow$ ✅ **`doonga`** (*"Tujhe sone doonga"*)
- ❌ `jaunga` $\rightarrow$ ✅ **`jaoonga`** (*"Main kal aaoonga"*)

### 🔤 5.3 Keyboard Case Sensitivity & Polymorphic Capitalization Mechanics
A frequent question when setting up mobile shortcuts is whether separate uppercase and lowercase shortcuts are required for sentence starts (e.g. `Muje` vs `muje`). Both Apple iOS and Google Gboard implement **polymorphic case propagation**, provided shortcuts are stored strictly in lowercase:

1. **Why All Shortcuts Are Strictly Lowercase**:
   - Every shortcut across `gboard_dictionary.zip`, `gboard_hinglish_shortcuts.txt`, `gboard_hinglish_shortcuts.csv`, `ios_text_replacement.plist`, and `ios_text_replacement.csv` is stored in **100% lowercase**.
   - If a shortcut were registered in title case (e.g. `Muje`), mobile keyboard trie algorithms treat the trigger as case-restricted, and typing lowercase `muje` in the middle of a sentence would fail to trigger.
2. **Dynamic Capitalization Expansion**:
   - **Mid-sentence (all lowercase)**: Typing `muje` $\rightarrow$ automatically expands to **`mujhe`**.
   - **Sentence start (Title Case)**: Typing `Muje` $\rightarrow$ the keyboard's auto-capitalization engine recognizes the sentence boundary and automatically expands to **`Mujhe`**.
   - **Intense emphasis (ALL CAPS)**: Typing `MUJE` $\rightarrow$ automatically expands to **`MUJHE`**.
3. **Zero Redundancy**: Because lowercase triggers dynamically preserve the user's typing case, you do not need duplicate entries or complex case rules.
